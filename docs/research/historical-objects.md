# Historical object research

Dates below describe the historical form or service, not one universal lifespan. The named museum objects are evidence-bearing examples, not claims that every example has the same design.

## Stereoscope and stereograph

**Name and dates.** Stereoscope (stereo viewer), used with a stereograph (a pair of views). Charles Wheatstone published a binocular stereoscopic device in 1838; David Brewster developed a compact lens stereoscope for public use by the 1851 Great Exhibition. Oliver Wendell Holmes described a comparatively inexpensive handheld viewer in 1861. There is no single “1859 stereoscope” that defines the technology: 1859 is the year Holmes published his influential essay, not the date of the Smithsonian Holmes example below.

**Maker/inventor.** Wheatstone demonstrated the principle; Brewster popularized a portable public viewer; Holmes refined an accessible handheld form. Attribute a surviving object to its catalogued maker, where known, rather than to the inventor of the whole category.

**Purpose and mechanism.** View paired photographs/drawings as a single apparently solid scene. Each eye sees a slightly different perspective through separate lenses; the brain fuses them into depth. Viewers vary: mirrors, prisms, lenses, supports, and card holders differ by design.

**Significance and afterlife.** Stereographs became mass visual entertainment and an at-home way to encounter distant places, architecture, news, and events. The format was commercially successful and evolved through later viewers and media; it was not a failed invention. Its binocular principle remains part of 3D imaging.

**Museum references.** Smithsonian NMAH, [Holmes Stereoscope, NMAH 994621](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_994621), dated “after 1861”; Smithsonian Gardens [hand-held stereoscope, HAC 1999.018](https://gardens.si.edu/collections/explore/object/hac_1999.018); Met, [Alex Beckers stereoscope, object 7901](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/7901). Met page identifies maker and materials; use its Open Access API record to confirm `isPublicDomain` before downloading.

## Zoetrope

**Name and dates.** William George Horner described a cylindrical animation device, the *Daedaleum*, in 1833/1834. The familiar commercial zoetrope was patented/marketed in the 1860s; William Ensign Lincoln’s U.S. patent was assigned to Milton Bradley, which sold the toy. Historians distinguish Horner’s conception from later commercial versions; do not credit Lincoln as the sole inventor of the principle.

**Maker/inventor.** Horner proposed the early device; Lincoln and Milton Bradley are associated with the US commercial form. Specific Smithsonian examples name Milton Bradley & Company as maker.

**Purpose and mechanism.** Entertainment and demonstration of apparent motion. A strip of sequential drawings sits inside a slotted rotating drum. Looking through the slots while it spins presents successive images in brief glimpses; persistence of vision and perceptual integration create the motion illusion. The slits interrupt the view and reduce blur.

**Significance and afterlife.** A popular optical toy and a key precursor in the history of animated moving images. Zoetropes remained commercially successful as toys and later reappeared in artistic and educational installations; their basic principle is still used to explain frame-based animation.

**Museum references.** Smithsonian NMAH [Zoetrope, PG.005087A](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_902717), [Zoetrope, PH.4228](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_902680), and [Milton Bradley zoetrope with 32 bands](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_760076). Oxford History of Science Museum, [Zoetrope history](https://www.mhs.ox.ac.uk/exhibits/fancy-names-and-fun-toys/zoetrope/). Peer-reviewed: Christine Veras, “Reanimating the History and the Forgotten Characteristics of the Zoetrope,” *Animation* (2022), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/17468477221085412).

## Tinfoil phonograph

**Name and dates.** Edison’s tinfoil phonograph, devised in 1877 and patented February 19, 1878. August 12, 1877 is often repeated as a completion date, but LOC notes evidence that the working model may have been built in December 1877. Prefer “1877; patent 1878” unless discussing that dating dispute.

**Inventor/maker.** Thomas A. Edison conceived and sketched the mechanism; machinist John Kruesi built the first machine from Edison’s sketch. This distinction is supported by the Library of Congress.

**Original purpose.** Edison’s work grew from experiments in recording/repeating telegraph signals and the question of whether telephone speech could be captured. He proposed dictation, telephone-message preservation, music, teaching, and other uses; the first model also became a public novelty and demonstration.

**Mechanism.** A hand-cranked cylinder covered in tinfoil rotates under a diaphragm and stylus. Sound vibrates the diaphragm; its stylus embosses a vertical “hill-and-dale” groove in the foil. On playback, the groove moves a stylus and diaphragm, reproducing sound. Foil was fragile and usually supported only a few playbacks; the device required skill to operate.

**Significance and afterlife.** First practical demonstration of recording and reproducing sound; it was an immediate public novelty success but not a durable consumer format. Edison paused work; wax-cylinder improvements by Bell and Tainter and then later Edison models made the tinfoil version obsolete. Its limitations led to redesign, not proof that sound recording failed.

**Museum references.** Smithsonian NMAH [Edison Tin Foil Phonograph, NMAH 855475](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_855475) and [Edison tin foil phonograph, NMAH 703497](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_703497), dated 1878. LOC, [History of the Cylinder Phonograph](https://www.loc.gov/static/collections/edison-company-motion-pictures-and-sound-recordings/articles-and-essays/history-of-edison-sound-recordings/history-of-the-cylinder-phonograph.html), and [Thomas Edison timeline](https://www.loc.gov/collections/edison-company-motion-pictures-and-sound-recordings/articles-and-essays/timeline/).

## Pneumatic postal tubes

**Name and dates.** Pneumatic dispatch/mail (postal tube) systems were used in European cities before US networks. London postal service began in 1863; Philadelphia was the first US city to adopt service in 1893. New York’s service began in 1897; it was interrupted nationwide in 1918, resumed in New York in 1922, and ended there on December 31, 1953. Always specify a city: there was no single system-wide end date.

**Inventor/manufacturer.** No single inventor of pneumatic conveyance. Birney Clark Batcheller engineered the principal American postal tube system and patented a process; private firms built and operated networks under contract. New York’s first system was associated with the Tubular Dispatch Company.

**Original purpose and mechanism.** Rapidly move letters and small parcels between busy urban post offices, railway terminals, and exchange points without street traffic. A sealed cylindrical carrier loaded with mail traveled through underground tubes under pressure or vacuum from station machinery. Switches and linked routes sent carriers between stations; clerks unloaded and sorted contents. Details and carrier sizes varied across cities and decades.

**Significance and afterlife.** It successfully moved large volumes quickly in dense cities. New York’s network grew to about 27 miles and carried millions of letters; pneumatic tubes were a serious part of the postal infrastructure. High operating/contract costs, fixed buried infrastructure, changing transport economics, and improved motor delivery contributed to closure. Pneumatic conveyance persists in other settings, including hospitals and retail operations.

**Museum references.** National Postal Museum [Pneumatic Tube Canister, NPM 0.269514.1](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/object/npm_0.269514.1), [Pneumatic Tube Canister spotlight](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/collections/object-spotlight/pneumatic-tube-canister), and [Pneumatic Tube Mail exhibition](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/customers-and-communities-serving-the-cities-city-free-delivery/pneumatic-tube-mail). LOC, [N.Y. Post Office—Pneumatic Tube, Bain 17144](https://www.loc.gov/item/2014697406/). Smithsonian Magazine’s synthesis, drawing on National Postal Museum curator Lynn Heidelbaugh and institutional records: [New York system history](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/when-a-labyrinth-of-pneumatic-tubes-shuttled-mail-beneath-the-streets-of-new-york-city-180983472/).

## Source and uncertainty notes

- Smithsonian and Met collection records are primary evidence for the specific object, catalogued date, material, maker, and rights indicators; records can change as cataloguing continues.
- The Oxford museum and the 2022 peer-reviewed zoetrope paper support Horner’s early proposal and the later commercial history. Their date framing differs slightly (1833 vs. 1834); report both as 1833/1834 rather than impose false precision.
- LOC’s Edison essay explicitly flags disagreement about the often-quoted August 12, 1877 date. Do not present that date as settled.
- The pneumatic system ended at different times in different places. New York’s 1953 closure must not be generalized to every city.
